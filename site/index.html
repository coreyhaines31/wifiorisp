Your call freezes mid-sentence. Again.

Your face is stuck on everyone else's screen. The Wi‑Fi icon shows full bars. Someone in the next room says the TV is buffering too. Is it the Wi‑Fi, the router, or your internet provider?

From your browser, all three look exactly the same: pages hang, calls stutter, uploads crawl. From your Mac, the way to tell them apart is to time your router and the internet at the same moment, while it's happening. That's the whole idea behind WiFi or ISP.

What you've probably tried

“The speed test says I'm fine.”

By the time you open it, the hiccup has passed. And a fast result doesn't say whether the slow part was your Wi‑Fi or your provider.

“I've restarted the router three times.”

Sometimes that helps. When the problem is past your router, it only takes the whole house offline for five minutes.

“My ISP says it's my Wi‑Fi.”

Support can't see your living room, so they start with your router. You need a log that shows your router kept answering while the internet didn't.

One question, answered with two numbers.

Every few seconds, the app times a round trip to your router and, at the same moment, one to the internet. Your router is the border: everything before it is yours, everything after it is your ISP's.

What to try first, second, and third.

Under every verdict is a short list called What to do: the next things to try, in the order worth trying them. Each one opens the right guide, starts a test, or exports your report.

The steps fit your setup. The app recognizes your router's maker and your provider. So “restart your router” opens the guide for your brand, and “check the outage page” opens Spectrum's or Verizon's, not a search box. Twelve large US providers have their own guides; everyone else gets the general steps.

Some limits no setting can fix, and the app says so plainly when it finds one. Your plan settings add two more: a plan too small for your home, and getting much less than you pay for.

  • Your router is holding your Mac back: your Mac supports Wi‑Fi 6E, but your router tops out at Wi‑Fi 5 or older.
  • Your Wi‑Fi is slower than your plan: speed tests hit what your Wi‑Fi link can carry, while you pay for a lot more.
  • Your router can't fix lag under load: the lag builds up in your modem or at your ISP. A router with smart queueing (SQM) could keep it in check; yours has none.

A flight recorder for your connection.

Slow internet never happens while the support rep is on the phone. So WiFi or ISP logs it in the background: signal, noise, transmit rate, roaming, router and internet round trips, and every drop. Look back an hour or a month.

When you're ready to complain, export a plain-text report. It lists each outage and whether your router kept answering through it, which is the part that makes an ISP stop telling you to restart your router.

Open History (⌘H) to see 1 hour, 6 hours, 24 hours, 7 days, or 30 days, with every drop marked and whose side it was on. Keep 7, 30, or 90 days of log, and export everything as CSV if you'd rather make your own charts.

How it decides whose side is slow.

No black box. Every number below is a constant in the app's source, and the full method is in how it works on GitHub.

Three round trips at once

Each check times a TCP handshake to your router (on port 53, 80, or 443, whichever it answers), and to 1.1.1.1 and 8.8.8.8 on port 443. The faster of the two internet replies counts, so one resolver having a bad moment isn't blamed on your ISP. A handshake is exactly one round trip and carries no data. No ping, no admin password.

Wi‑Fi readings, never a scan

From macOS it reads signal and noise, transmit rate, channel, band, channel width, and Wi‑Fi generation for the network you're on. It never scans for nearby networks.

A verdict from the last few minutes

It looks at the last six checks from the last three minutes, and waits for at least three. Behind a VPN it still times the Wi‑Fi router underneath. Over Ethernet it skips the signal and compares your router with the internet.

Where speed tests win

A trusted throughput number against a huge server network, and fast.com needs no install at all. Use one when you want to know how fast. Use WiFi or ISP when you want to know whose fault slow is. (It has a speed test too, against M-Lab.)

Where macOS's tools win

networkQuality uses the same IETF responsiveness method as the lag test here, with no install. Wireless Diagnostics shows far more radio detail than any menu bar app, with scans and a packet sniffer. Neither keeps watching after you close it.

Where router apps win

They test from the router itself, which separates your provider from your Wi‑Fi better than any app on your Mac can. They also see every device on your network. They only work with their own brand, and the history is often a subscription.

Where iStat Menus wins

Everything about your Mac: CPU, memory, disks, sensors, and per-app bandwidth. It can ping and tell you the internet is down; it doesn't say why. Run both: how they compare.

No tracking. No scanning. No password.

No tracking

The app has no analytics and no account, and your history stays on your Mac. It asks Cloudflare once per network which provider you’re on, to tailor its advice; otherwise it only talks to the internet for the tests you start.

Location, only for a name

macOS treats your Wi-Fi network's name as location data. Allow it and your history is labeled by network. Skip it and everything else still works.

Never scans

It reads your current connection without scanning for networks, and backs off if macOS's Wi-Fi service gets busy.

No admin rights

Round trips are timed with ordinary connections, not ping, so there's nothing to install with a password.

Every permission it asks for

  • Location: only to read your Wi‑Fi network's name. It never reads where you are.
  • Local network (macOS 15 and later): to time your router. It doesn't look for or connect to anything else.
  • Notifications: for the alerts you leave on.
  • Open at login: a standard login item, so it keeps watching after a restart. Turn it off in Settings → General.

Everything it sends over the network

  • A TCP handshake to your router, 1.1.1.1, and 8.8.8.8 every 5 to 30 seconds, with no data in it.
  • One request to Cloudflare per network to learn your provider's name, skipped on a VPN.
  • Tests you start: M-Lab for speed and Cloudflare for lag under load. M-Lab publishes every result, including your IP address, and the app tells you so before your first test.
  • An update check, which you can turn off.

What it never does

  • No analytics, no account, no crash reporter.
  • No scanning for nearby networks, and no ping or admin rights.
  • Nothing listens on the network: the wifiorisp command talks to the app through a file only your user account can open.
  • History stays in ~/Library/Application Support/WiFi or ISP/ until you export it.

Questions

Why is my Wi-Fi so slow?

Usually one of three things: a weak signal (too far from the router, or walls in the way), a crowded channel or overloaded router, or a problem past your router at your ISP. Each one looks the same from your browser. WiFi or ISP tells them apart by timing your router and the internet together.

My Mac's internet broke after weeks without a restart. Is it my Wi-Fi?

Maybe not. On macOS 26, a Mac awake for about 49.7 days without a restart can stop opening new connections: pages and uploads time out while ping still works. Only restarting the Mac fixes it. When that's the likely cause, WiFi or ISP tells you to restart your Mac before it blames your Wi-Fi or ISP. Why it happens.

What does “WiFi” mean in the verdict?

Your side of the line: your Mac's radio, the air, and your router. “ISP” means everything past the router, including your modem and the line to your provider. If your router is slow, that's on your side, even with a strong signal.

What is bufferbloat?

When an upload or download fills your connection, packets pile up in a queue and every round trip slows down. That's why video calls stutter while a backup runs. The lag-under-load test measures it and says whether the queue is in your Wi-Fi or router, or past it. Turning on Smart Queue Management (SQM) on your router usually fixes it.

What do dBm and ms mean?

You don't need to know: the app says Excellent, Fast, or Slow. If you're curious, dBm is radio signal strength (−30 is as strong as it gets, −80 barely works) and ms is milliseconds, how long a reply takes (your router should answer in a few). Turn on the number in the menu bar in Settings if you like it.

Does it run speed tests all the time?

No. The background checks are single tiny round trips, every 5 to 30 seconds. Speed and lag tests only run when you click them.

Why does it ask for Location?

Only to show your Wi-Fi network's name, which macOS treats as location data. The app never asks where you are. Say no and it shows “Unknown network”.

Does it work over Ethernet or a VPN?

Yes. On a cable it skips the signal readings and still compares your router with the internet. With a VPN on, it still measures the Wi-Fi network underneath.

How is this different from a speed test?

A speed test measures how much your connection carries, end to end, at the moment you run it. WiFi or ISP times your router and the internet at the same moment, all day, so it can say which side is slow, including the times you weren't watching. It has a speed test too, for checking what you get against your plan.

Does it use much battery?

No. The background checks are three tiny connections every 5 to 30 seconds, it never scans for networks, and it stops while your Mac sleeps. When I measured it in November 2026, it used under 0.15% of one CPU core.

Which Macs does it run on?

macOS 14 Sonoma or later, on Apple silicon and Intel. It's signed and notarized by Apple, and it updates itself with Sparkle; if you installed it with Homebrew, brew upgrades it.

How do I uninstall it?

Turn off Open at login in Settings, quit it from its menu, and drag it from Applications to the Trash. To remove its history and settings too, run brew uninstall --zap --cask wifiorisp, or delete the WiFi or ISP folder in ~/Library/Application Support.

Is it really free?

Yes. The source is on GitHub under the Functional Source License, which turns into the MIT license two years after each release.
