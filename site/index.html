One question, answered with two numbers.

Every few seconds, the app times a round trip to your router and, at the same moment, one to the internet. Your router is the border: everything before it is yours, everything after it is your ISP's.

A flight recorder for your connection.

Slow internet never happens while the support rep is on the phone. So WiFi or ISP logs it in the background: signal, noise, transmit rate, roaming, router and internet round trips, and every drop. Look back an hour or a month.

When you're ready to complain, export a plain-text report. It lists each outage and whether your router kept answering through it, which is the part that makes an ISP stop telling you to restart your router.

Know the moment it happens.

Get a heads-up when the internet drops (and when it's back, with how long it was gone), when your Mac quietly falls back to 2.4 GHz, or when it roams to an access point that's worse than the one it left.

Turn each one off in Settings. Reconnecting after sleep never counts as a drop.

Lags under load: past your router (bufferbloat)

Fills your connection both ways and measures how slow round trips get, using the IETF responsiveness test. Router probes during the test show whether the queue is on your side or past it, and what to change.

Light on your Mac. Silent about you.

No tracking

No analytics, no account. History stays on your Mac, and nothing is sent anywhere except the tests you start.

Location, only for a name

macOS treats your Wi-Fi network's name as location data. Allow it and your history is labeled by network. Skip it and everything else still works.

Never scans

It reads your current connection without scanning for networks, and backs off if macOS's Wi-Fi service gets busy.

No admin rights

Round trips are timed with ordinary connections, not ping, so there's nothing to install with a password.

Questions

Why is my Wi-Fi so slow?

Usually one of three things: a weak signal (too far from the router, or walls in the way), a crowded channel or overloaded router, or a problem past your router at your ISP. Each one looks the same from your browser. WiFi or ISP tells them apart by timing your router and the internet together.

What does “WiFi” mean in the verdict?

Your side of the line: your Mac's radio, the air, and your router. “ISP” means everything past the router, including your modem and the line to your provider. If your router is slow, that's on your side, even with a strong signal.

What is bufferbloat?

When an upload or download fills your connection, packets pile up in a queue and every round trip slows down. That's why video calls stutter while a backup runs. The lag-under-load test measures it and says whether the queue is in your Wi-Fi or router, or past it. Turning on Smart Queue Management (SQM) on your router usually fixes it.

What do dBm and ms mean?

You don't need to know: the app says Excellent, Fast, or Slow. If you're curious, dBm is radio signal strength (−30 is as strong as it gets, −80 barely works) and ms is milliseconds, how long a reply takes (your router should answer in a few). Turn on the number in the menu bar in Settings if you like it.

Does it run speed tests all the time?

No. The background checks are single tiny round trips, every 5 to 30 seconds. Speed and lag tests only run when you click them.

Why does it ask for Location?

Only to show your Wi-Fi network's name, which macOS treats as location data. The app never asks where you are. Say no and it shows “Unknown network”.

Does it work over Ethernet or a VPN?

Yes. On a cable it skips the signal readings and still compares your router with the internet. With a VPN on, it still measures the Wi-Fi network underneath.

Is it really free?

Yes. The source is on GitHub under the Functional Source License, which turns into the MIT license two years after each release.
